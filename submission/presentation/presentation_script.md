# Presentation script

Good afternoon. This project asks a practical model-selection question: as Korean sentiment labels grow from a few hundred reviews to the full cleaned training pool, when does a learned-from-scratch neural model become worth its extra cost? The short answer is that the crossover depends strongly on the domain.

A full-data leaderboard cannot answer this on its own. With 200 labels, a sparse TF-IDF model can estimate useful word associations immediately. A neural model must learn its token representations and its classifier from the same small sample. At full data, that neural model has a better chance to exploit sequence patterns, but training time, latency, memory, and model size become part of the decision.

I split the work into two parts. Part 1 is the approved course project: three Korean review datasets, nine complete pipelines, and learning curves from 200 labels to the full cleaned pool. Part 2 is a separate bonus extension. It asks whether labels that already exist in two source domains can reduce the need for new labels in the third. I did not add continual learning or pretrained language models.

The three domains are NSMC movie reviews, Naver Shopping reviews, and Korean Steam reviews. After cleaning, the fixed splits remain nearly balanced. The important fairness control is the subset design. For each seed, I create one deterministic stratified order of the training pool. A smaller set is a strict prefix of every larger set, and all nine models receive exactly the same example IDs. Validation and test examples never enter those prefixes.

The classical family contains logistic regression, linear SVM, Random Forest, and XGBoost over the same word unigram and bigram TF-IDF representation. The neural family contains average learned embeddings plus an MLP, LSTM, GRU, a small Transformer, and a small Mamba classifier. Neural representations are learned from scratch. Every dataset-model pair gets ten predefined tuning candidates at 8,000 labels. I select by validation macro-F1 with cost tie-breaks, then freeze that configuration across final sizes and seeds.

Macro-F1 is the primary metric because it gives the negative and positive classes equal weight. I define a crossover before looking at the final story. At a given size, the best neural mean must exceed the best TF-IDF mean by at least 0.01. A sustained crossover must also satisfy that criterion at every larger tested size. Every final result uses seeds 13, 42, and 2026. With only three seeds, I report means, sample standard deviations, and paired effects, but I do not make significance claims.

These are the complete Part 1 learning curves. TF-IDF SVM or logistic regression leads at the small and medium sizes in all three domains. Neural curves rise more sharply near the largest budgets. NSMC shows the clearest change. Shopping keeps a strong sparse baseline even when its neural models become competitive, and Steam has a tight cluster of neural methods at the end.

The difference plot gives the main result. NSMC first reaches the one-point threshold at 64,000 labels, and the advantage remains above the threshold at full data. Shopping does not reach a sustained crossover through 159,918 labels. Steam does not reach one through 79,828. Neural models do lead at full data in all three domains, but the advantage clears the prespecified practical margin only on NSMC.

That gain has a real cost. Comparing the strongest full-data model in each family, the neural fit takes 46.5 times as long on NSMC, 48.7 times on Shopping, and 27.7 times on Steam. The corresponding macro-F1 gains are 1.77, 0.81, and 0.85 points. The timing is a practical mixed-device comparison: TF-IDF used the CPU and neural models used CUDA. It should not be interpreted as an architecture-only microbenchmark. For a team choosing a baseline, linear TF-IDF remains difficult to beat on cost.

The low-label neural results motivate the bonus question. In each rotation, the target supplies 200, 500, 1,000, or 2,000 new labels. The two source domains each supply 2,000 labels that are assumed to exist already. Any savings would therefore mean new target-domain labels saved. It would not mean total annotation savings.

I compare five methods. The best Part 1 TF-IDF model and the Part 1 target-only Transformer are reused. The new runs are pooled cross-entropy, pooled standard supervised contrastive learning, and pooled DB-CDSupCon. The three new objectives share examples, the Transformer backbone, balanced sampling, optimizer rules, and seeds. Pooled CE is the necessary baseline because all contrastive methods also benefit from seeing the source data.

DB-CDSupCon changes the contrastive positive weights. Each batch balances the six domain-label cells. Same-label examples from another domain receive weight alpha, while same-label examples from the same domain retain weight one. The contrastive projection head is used only for training. Deployment bypasses it, so the deployed classifier is identical in size and inference path to pooled CE.

The bonus curves show two effects of different sizes. Pooling source data gives a large improvement over the weak target-only Transformer. At 200 labels, the gains are 12.05 macro-F1 points on NSMC, 5.19 on Shopping, and 13.54 on Steam. The comparison with TF-IDF is harder: pooled CE is 2.20 points higher on NSMC, 2.81 points lower on Shopping, and 2.21 points higher on Steam. Standard SupCon and DB-CDSupCon move above and below pooled CE depending on the target and label count. There is no consistent contrastive winner.

That mixed pattern prevents a numeric label-savings claim. Some pooled methods cross the TF-IDF reference at 200 labels on NSMC and Steam. The target-only Transformer never reaches the threshold anywhere from 200 to 2,000 labels. Savings require both thresholds, because the calculation subtracts the candidate crossover from the target-only crossover. The honest result is that savings are not estimable within this grid. That does not mean the transfer benefit is zero.

The secondary diagnostics tell the same mixed story. Contrastive training sometimes improves target and source performance together, and sometimes trades one for the other. DB-CDSupCon improves the average cross-domain separation gap only for Shopping. Its fit-time overhead ranges from 1.08 to 1.41 times pooled CE. The projection head is training-only, so deployed parameters and inference stay unchanged.

The controlled benchmark, the NSMC crossover, and the benefit from pooling are the stable findings. A universal crossover is not supported, and neither contrastive objective dominates pooled CE. The limits are also important: three seeds, three binary review domains, learned-from-scratch models only, and timing that depends on the recorded CPU and GPU.

I would use four rules from this study. Start with linear TF-IDF when labels are scarce. Test a small neural model near the larger label budgets because crossover is domain-specific. Price the last fraction of macro-F1 against the deployment constraints. For cross-domain transfer, establish pooled cross-entropy before adding a contrastive loss. Thank you.
